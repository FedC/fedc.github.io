*As artificial intelligence transforms how we build software, the value of human connection, trust, and understanding deserves a closer look.*

There's an interesting conversation happening among software engineers right now. And it's not just about whether AI will replace our jobs.

It's about what makes us valuable in the first place.

Over the past few years, we've watched artificial intelligence go from helping developers autocomplete a few lines of code to generating entire applications, designing interfaces, writing tests, and suggesting architectural decisions.

The pace is extraordinary. Things that once required days of development can sometimes be accomplished in hours.

As someone who builds software for a living, I find that exciting. But it also raises questions about where our profession is heading.

I've had conversations with other software engineers and mentors of mine who are asking themselves similar questions. How do we continue to make ourselves relevant? What do we bring to the table when the tools become increasingly capable of doing the work themselves?

One perspective has stayed with me: **people still want someone they trust to help them navigate technology.**

And I think there's something important in that.

## It usually starts with a conversation

When someone approaches me with an idea, they rarely arrive with a perfectly defined technical specification.

Sometimes it's a business owner who wants a better website. Sometimes it's someone imagining an application that doesn't exist yet. Other times, it's a company trying to solve a problem with systems that no longer work well together.

The first step isn't opening an editor or asking AI to generate a solution.

It's understanding what they're trying to accomplish.

What problem are we solving? Who is this for? What already exists? What would make this successful? Is the original idea even the best way to approach it?

These conversations often change the direction of a project before a single line of code is written.

And that's a part of software development I've always enjoyed.

## Knowing how to build something isn't the same as knowing what to build

AI has made it remarkably easy to turn an idea into something functional.

But just because we *can* build something doesn't necessarily mean we *should*, or that the first implementation is the right one.

A client might ask for a complicated custom application when a simpler integration would solve the problem. Someone might want to automate a process without realizing the implications for privacy or security. Another person might have an ambitious concept that needs to be broken into smaller, more practical steps.

The valuable response isn't always, "Yes, I can build that."

Sometimes it's, "Yes, but I think there's a better way."

Or even, "Before we build anything, let's make sure we're solving the right problem."

AI can offer these recommendations too, and it's getting better at doing so. But there's a difference between receiving an answer and working with someone who understands your goals, your limitations, and the consequences of getting something wrong.

## Trust is more than technical competence

One of the most interesting things about this moment in technology is that technical capabilities are becoming more accessible to everyone.

You no longer need years of programming experience to experiment with building software.

That's a remarkable development. I think it opens the door for more people to create things that previously felt out of reach.

But accessibility doesn't automatically create confidence.

When a business owner makes a decision about their website, customer data, payments, or internal operations, they're often making a decision that affects their livelihood.

They want to know that someone understands what's at stake.

They want to ask questions without feeling like they should already know the answers.

They want someone who can explain the tradeoffs, communicate when something isn't working, and help them make informed decisions.

And perhaps most importantly, they want to know who they can turn to when things don't go according to plan.

That relationship isn't simply a technical service. It's built through communication, transparency, consistency, and accountability.

## The role of the engineer is evolving

I don't believe the answer is to resist AI or insist that humans will always be better at every part of software development.

That would be difficult to defend, especially given how quickly the technology is progressing.

I use AI in my own development process. It helps me explore ideas, work through problems, accelerate implementation, and experiment with approaches I might not otherwise have considered.

It's changing how I work, and I welcome that.

But I also think it pushes us to reconsider where we focus our attention.

Less time spent on repetitive implementation can mean more time thinking about the experience we're creating, understanding the people using it, and considering how different parts of a system fit together.

It can create room for creativity, experimentation, and deeper collaboration.

Of course, none of that happens automatically. Faster tools can just as easily produce more complexity, more noise, and more software nobody really needs.

The tools don't decide what deserves our attention. We do.

## What happens when AI becomes better at giving advice, too?

There's an uncomfortable but worthwhile question here.

What if AI becomes just as capable of understanding business needs, identifying risks, and recommending solutions as the experienced engineers we rely on today?

It's entirely possible that some of the advantages we currently associate with human expertise will become less distinctive over time.

I don't think we should ignore that possibility.

But I also wonder whether technical capability and human relationships are necessarily interchangeable.

We already have access to extraordinary amounts of information, yet we still seek out people whose judgment we respect. We consult colleagues, mentors, friends, and specialists, not always because they possess information we couldn't find elsewhere, but because we value their perspective and the relationship we've built with them.

Perhaps our relationship with AI will evolve in ways that challenge even that distinction.

For now, though, I see technology becoming more capable while the need for thoughtful human collaboration remains very real.

## Building with people, not just for them

I've always been drawn to the creative side of engineering: taking an idea, exploring what it could become, and gradually turning it into something useful, intuitive, and sometimes unexpected.

AI makes that process more accessible and, in many ways, more exciting.

But the part I find most rewarding is still working with people.

Listening to their ideas. Understanding their challenges. Offering a different perspective. Finding a simpler solution. Occasionally questioning the original plan.

And then building something together that makes sense.

I don't know exactly what software development will look like five or ten years from now. I doubt anyone does.

But as the technology changes, I think it's worth remembering that software has never existed in isolation.

**Behind every meaningful product is a human problem, a human ambition, or a human experience worth improving.**

And that's where I still like to begin.
